# Training-free motion statistics match self-supervised behavior encoders

## Summary

arXiv:2611.24792v1 Announce Type: cross 
Abstract: Self-supervised encoders for animal behavior are often trained on, or fed, hand-built movement features. We ask whether they beat those ingredients when compared fairly. A training-free representation, made of generic per-frame measurements summarized over fixed time windows and reduced with PCA, is compared with retrained and released encoders (BAMS, hBehaveMAE) under a preregistered protocol: frozen representations of equal size and one shared classifier. On CalMS21 it reaches 85.8 macro F1 against 82.4 for the best encoder, ties with four labelled videos, and leads when the annotator changes or new behaviors must be learned from a few videos; the reading holds for encoders retrained with three seeds. On the MABe22 mouse triplets it scores 83.7 against 80.3 for the released hBehaveMAE (gap +3.4, 95% interval +2.1 to +4.7). On a second species, the MABe22 fly groups, the same recipe scores 51.6 mean F1 over 50 tasks against 48.2 for BAMS, the best published method. On Shot7M2, the synthetic basketball benchmark built by hBehaveMAE's authors, it scores 68.8 against 64.7 for hBehaveMAE, the best method in that paper, though hBehaveMAE remains better on the shortest movements. Short time windows carry most of the result. Raw keypoints in each body's own frame, summarized over the same windows, already match or beat the encoders on both mouse datasets and on Shot7M2; on the fly groups they fall short, and the measurements add 5.3 points. On these benchmarks, current behavior encoders have yet to beat the hand-built features they learn from, and such baselines should be reported alongside them.
