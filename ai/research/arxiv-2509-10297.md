# Beyond Moral Performance: Empirical Assessment of LLMs' Moral Competence Through Judgment Stability

## Summary

arXiv:2509.10297v2 Announce Type: replace 
Abstract: Evaluations of large language models (LLMs) have largely measured moral performance, whether their outputs are morally appropriate, rather than moral competence, the capacity to judge on the basis of morally relevant considerations. We test for competence by varying morally irrelevant features of moral dilemmas. Because moral dilemmas rarely admit a single correct answer, we assess the stability of framework preferences, a model's relative ordering of five normative ethical frameworks, rather than of verdicts. We test 16 LLM configurations, reasoning and non-reasoning, on 18 dilemmas in six thematic domains for reliability across repeated runs and stability across prompt length, thematic domain, and reporting format. The configurations share broadly the same preference ordering: libertarianism falls far below the other frameworks in all 16, and care and virtue ethics occupy the top two positions in 15. This ordering is highly reliable across runs and largely preserved across reporting formats, and we find no evidence that it depends on thematic domain. Prompt length, however, affects it: longer prompts lower the scores of libertarian, virtue ethics, and care ethics outcomes but not those of utilitarian and deontological outcomes, so that with long prompts care ethics, utilitarianism, and virtue ethics can no longer be distinguished, while libertarianism remains last. Reasoning configurations show closer agreement between reporting formats but less reliability in rankings. Since human moral judgement also shows sensitivity to morally irrelevant features, perfect stability cannot be the criterion for competence; on balance, the pattern of our data remains more compatible with the existence of moral competence than with its absence, with the sensitivity to prompt length as the main qualification.
