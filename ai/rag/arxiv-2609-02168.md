# FUSE: A Modular Framework for Evaluating Dangerous Capabilities of LLMs

## Summary

arXiv:2609.02168v2 Announce Type: replace 
Abstract: Dangerous capabilities of large language models (LLMs) cannot yet be compared across models: knowledge, refusal, and harm benchmarks each cover one step of a misuse path, on different models, protocols, and scales, so regulators cannot tell which model poses greater risk. We present FUSE, a modular framework that defines dangerous capability as a model's ability to advance a black-box adversary along a misuse path, and measures three stages of that path under one protocol: hazardous knowledge, estimated by item response theory; defense under multi-turn adversarial induction by a red-team agent; and the actionability of content produced once the model complies (harm). The stages form a fixed profile, not a scalar score, whose harm entry pairs how often a model complies with how severe its output is. New danger domains plug in as four-asset modules without changing the pipeline. On twelve commercial LLMs in the chemical-biological domain, the stages diverge: models with comparable knowledge differ markedly in defense, strong defenders do not produce less actionable content when they comply, and Claude, DeepSeek, and GPT occupy distinct regions of the profile. Across 41 months of releases, dangerous capability has not monotonically declined: knowledge rises within every family, defense diverges by family, and conditional severity does not fall. A tool-enhanced cybersecurity pilot on three models shows the same divergence between knowledge and defense. Three domain experts agree closely among themselves (ICC 0.91 for defense, 0.85 for harm); the judge preserves their ordering (Spearman 0.78 and 0.63) but scores 23.8 and 18.4 points higher on a 100-point scale, so we use it to rank models, not to measure absolute risk.
